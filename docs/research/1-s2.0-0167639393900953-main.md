## **1. Introduction** 

NOISEX-92 consists of a specification for an experiment and a database on which to carry out the experiment. It was produced under the auspices of the NATO Research Study Group on Speech Processing (RSG.10) which is carrying out a programme of work examining and assessing the performance of automatic speech recognition systems in adverse/noisy environments. NOISEX-92 is the result of an initial part of this programme. It has been placed on two CD-ROMs (approximately 1.4 Gbytes of data) which are openly available. 

The aim of the RSG.10 programme is to address some of the problems of quantifying the 

effects of noisy environments on modern automatic speech recognition systems and to stimulate dialogue on future assessment work in this area, e.g. on suitable future databases and experiments. To this end the RSG.10 laboratories (in liaison with the ESPRIT SAM Project laboratories) have developed the NOISEX-92 study, which is intended to provide common data with which to work in the short term as well as to highlight future requirements. It does not address the full range of factors affecting speech recognition in noisy environments; the data is for instance artificial in nature because the speech and noise have been added together. NOISEX-92 does, however, provide a set of control data that can be easily used and for which comparative experimental re-

The database is made up of a set of speech recordings made in a quiet environment _added_ to recordings of eight different noises at various signal-to-noise ratios. The experimental specification defines a partitioning of the data for training and testing. A calibration protocol is defined together with various other tests designed to exercise the robustness of speech recognizers. 

# **2. The speech material** 

The speech material is derived from a portion of that on the ESPRIT SAM EUROM_0, it consists of tables of read digits. A key aspect of this database is that it is designed for use with continuous recognition systems, i.e. the data is not pre-segrnented but presented in the form of sequences of continuously recorded sets of utterances. Therefore, the data should not be used in a pre-segmented form; labelling and annotation data is provided for training and scoring purposes only. The data is partitioned into training and test sets. 

The original EUROM_0 data was sampled at 16 kHz and stored as 16-bit integers, it was prepared for use in this database by an offset and scaling process. Here, any DC offset was removed and an attenuation was applied to enable the addition of the noise without causing an overflow of the 16-bit integer range. 

The speech consists of data spoken by one male and one female speaker. Each spoke two tables of a hundred isolated digits, one table can be used as training data, the other is test data. The recording of each table lasts for about five minutes and is contained in a single continuous file. Similarly, there are two tables of fifty-digit triplets spoken by each speaker, one for training and one for testing. Each of these recordings lasts for approximately three minutes and is contained in a single file. 

Two additional versions of the speech data are supplied; one set attenuated by 12 dB and a set with a spectral tilt (a flat frequency response up to 250 Hz and 3 dB/octave tilt above 250 Hz) 

applied. These data are provided for a very minimalist examination of recognizer robustness to some of the typical effects of speaking in noise, i.e. level and spectral variation. 

# **3. The noise material** 

The noises are taken from the RSG.10 NOISE-ROM-0 (Steeneken and Geurtsen, 1988); the twentyfour noises of NOISE-ROM-0 were selected in order to offer as wide as possible variation in characteristics. A subset of eight of these was selected for NOISEX-92 on the basis of problem/application relevance and difficulty for recognizers (e.g. non-stationarity). The noises used are stationary long-term average-speech spectrum noise (no. 6), machine-gun noise (no. 16), STITEL babble (no. 18), Lynx-helicopter cockpit noise (no. 12), F16 fast-jet cockpit noise (no. 20), car noise (no. 23), factory noise (no. 21) and office noise (no. 14). 

The original NOISE-ROM-0 data was sampled at 20 kHz and stored as 16-bit integers, it was prepared for use in this database by downsampling it to 16 kHz and applying an attenuation. The attenuation was applied to enable the addition of the noise without causing an overflow of the 16-bit integer range. 

# **4. The speech-in-noise material** 

The level of the speech and noise records were measured separately using the Speech Level Meter (Steeneken and Houtgast, 1986), scaling factors were then calculated for each recording to give all the recordings equal level (defined as the "nominal" level). Hence, addition of nominal level "clean" speech and noise recordings gives a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 0 dB. Since the machine-gun noise is impulsive in nature the level correction of this signal was obtained from the peak level. 

The speech-in-noise signals were created by adding the pre-scaled speech and noise files together at various SNRs. The SNR was varied in 6 dB steps; positive SNRs were obtained by scaling the noise signal by successive factors of 0.5, while

All the speech, both training and test data sets, have all the noises added at all the SNRs. The same noise data was added to both the training and the test speech data, i.e. there was no partitioning of the noise data into training and test sets. 

The "clean" versions of the speech and noise recordings are supplied and can be used to create additional datasets as required, e.g. other SNRs or for different types of experiments. For example, a partitioning of the noise data into training and test sets is defined for the creation of special versions of the database for the assessment of speech enhancement systems. 

# **5. The NOISEX-92 experiments** 

Various experiments are defined, the primary NOISEX-92 experiment is to train on the clean speech and then perform recognition on the speech-in-noise test data. Alternatively, a speechin-noise (at 6 dB SNR) training condition is specified. 

Training data is supplied, however, recognizers have different modes of operation (i.e. speaker dependent, speaker adaptive and speaker independent) and also varying amounts of training data may be required. The training data is available, but it is not necessary to use it; the aim is to use the recognizer in the most appropriate manner. 

The first test condition is speech without added noise (i.e. the recognizer baseline performance), then the 18 dB SNR followed by decreasing SNRs. 

The sets of spectrally tilted data and attenuated data can be used for both training as well as to assess the robustness of recognizers to such variations. 

# **6. Example experimental results** 

Various recognizers have been assessed on the NOISEX-92 database (Varga et al., 1992). The results reported here are for only the isolated 

digit portion of the experiment. The following performance assessments have been carried out: (1) Human performance, two listeners have been "assessed". 

- (2) A commercial speaker independent isolated digit recognizer, used as the hardware reference standard within the SAM project (no training required). 

- (3) A speaker dependent software recognizer using the noise masking technique of noise compensation (Varga and Moore, 1990) (trained on the clean speech data only). 

- (4) A speaker dependent software recognizer using the hidden Markov model based decomposition technique of noise compensation (Varga and Ponting, 1989) (trained on the clean speech data only). 

The presentation and comparison of the results for even this small portion of the NOISEX-92 experiment is a problem because of the large number of results that can be obtained. Various methods of presentation are illustrated here, two example performance (accuracy) curves are given, both on the male test data, one for the SAM reference hardware recognizer and one for the noise masking software recognizer. An overall comparison of recognizer performance on a database such as this is difficult; appropriate presentation of results can depend on the particular aspects of performance that are of interest. ## **7. Calibration** 

The problem of ensuring comparability of resuits and checking for errors can be addressed in several ways. A 1 kHz tone is provided in NOISEX-92 to calibrate analogue channels, but the RSG.10 group have also used the reference hardware recognizer of the SAM project as a calibration and checking mechanism. Results obtained by various laboratories are given in Table 1. The experimental variation is clear, however a correlation analysis (a crude measure) shows that the differences are not significant.

# **8. Conclusions, future databases and experiments** 

NOISEX-92 has proved to be very successful and is being used widely. In the light of the experience gained with NOISEX-92, consideration is now being given to the next phase of the NOISEX project. Despite its size NOISEX-92 contains only a limited amount on speech, 500 digits from each of the two speakers. The relatively large size of the database is due to the combinatorial explosion of the various signal-tonoise ratios and different noises. A possible way forward is not to provide the data pre-mixed; but rather to provide the original unmixed data together with a specification for the mixing process and perhaps software to carry out the mixing. This would add considerably to the complexity and work involved in running experiments and there is also higher potential for errors to occur in this process. However, this may be the only practicable solution if large quantities of speech are to be used. 

A range of options exist for future speech data; from diagnostic vocabularies which could be used to predict performance (see for example Part I (Steeneken and Varga, 1993)), through the use of other existing well known databases, to the generation of new more realistic databases. It will be desirable to partition the future databases into development and independent test data. The noise data should also be partitioned into training, optimisation and test data.
