What this page is

A working model of the ANVIL prototype: 28 parts at the sizes their datasheets give, the cables that join them, and both lanes running as real signal-processing code underneath. Nothing here is a video of a system — the numbers in the readout are computed on this page, frame by frame, from the signals drawn in the monitor above.

Reading the bench

The surface is marked into four zones, which are the four groups in the hardware document: the headset on its measurement mannequin, Lane A (the ear DSP, its manager and the amplifier), Lane B (the audio interface and the Jetson), and the communication unit. A part is clickable everywhere — in the viewport, in the cable list, and from the chain strips.

Only the signal spine is drawn. Thirteen of the twenty-five cables carry the path a reader has to follow; power, I²C, TDM and the rest appear under All cables. Each cable is matte sleeving with a coloured band at the ends for what it carries, because twenty-five coloured cables at once is unreadable.

They cannot be fused. A network that needs tens of milliseconds cannot place anti-noise at the ear in half a millisecond, which is why Lane A is separate hardware that keeps working when the Jetson is not.

What the simulation actually computes

At 16 kHz in 256-sample frames, against the wall clock. Levels are carried in pascals (1 Pa = 94 dB SPL), so every figure in the readout is a sound-pressure number for the signals on this page.

The gunshot run

Fire a shot traces one shot through the whole system in slow motion — 200 ms of real time spread over 20 seconds at the default rate — while the monitor, the chain strips, the log and the results card follow it. It is the same code as the live bench: a second copy of the machine is given four seconds of street noise to let the canceller settle, then the shot, all with a fixed random seed so the figures repeat run after run.

What the viewport shows during the run

The bench folds into the headset — every board and cable is something the headset carries — and the view moves to a range drawn to scale, with one grid square to the metre. The round leaves the rifle, its shock wave trails behind it as a cone, and the muzzle blast spreads as a sphere at the speed of sound. Both fronts are drawn from the same clock as the monitor, so the cone reaches the right earcup at 45.2 ms and the sphere at 87.5 ms, exactly when the traces below move. A card appears at each arrival with the figures measured in that run, and then the bench unfolds again. Any view button, or Back to the live bench, ends the sequence at once.

The range is to scale; the objects on it are not. The headset keeps its bench size, which is ten times too large for a 30 m range, and the rifle and the round are drawn oversize for the same reason. The crack is drawn as the run computes it: leaving the round's path at closest approach and crossing the 3 m at the speed of sound. That is a cone of 22.7°. The textbook Mach cone for this round is 24.7° and would arrive 0.8 ms sooner.

The crack arrives 42.3 ms before the blast because the bullet outruns the sound of its own muzzle. That gap is the only real look-ahead a headset ever gets, and the crack guard is built to use it: the run shows it firing within a fraction of a millisecond of the crack, so hear-through is already down when the blast lands.

What the run measures

Everything after the microphones: when the capsule overloads (the IM73A135 is rated to 135 dB, so the crack clips it and the A1 flag holds the anti-noise), when the crack guard fires, what the system plays into the ear during the blast, how much of the blast the canceller removes, the peak at the ear against 140 dBP, which Lane B frames are flagged as impulses, the compute time of every frame, and the Impulse Recovery Time of the raw boom microphone and of the Lane B output — computed with the definition in synthetic_generator/metrics/irt.py: octave bands from 125 Hz to 8 kHz, a 5 ms envelope, a baseline from the 150 ms before the loudest sample, and recovery once the envelope stays within 1/0.9 of that baseline for 20 ms.

The boom microphone's own overload point is taken as 130 dB. That is an assumption for a generic electret, not a datasheet figure, and it is the one number in the run that should be replaced once the boom is bought.

How the SNR figures are obtained

The clean speech and the noise are kept separately inside the simulation, so the same mask the mixture receives is also applied to each part on its own. The two energies are then compared over about two seconds. The improvement is measured on this page, not asserted — and it is the improvement of a classical mask, not of the trained network.

Two frames are left out of that average, for the same reasons the project's own metrics leave them out. Frames with no speech in them have no signal-to-noise ratio to report. Frames containing a gunshot — and the 130 ms after one — are excluded as well: a 157 dB blast against a 96 dB voice gives a number near −50 dB that says nothing about the enhancement. Those events are counted instead, on the impulses seen line, and they are what the impulse measures in synthetic_generator/ exist to judge. Typical readings here: about +8 dB of SNR improvement on the street scene and +10 dB between shots in the gunfire scene.

The cancellation figure the readout shows — typically 20–28 dB on these scenes — is what the canceller achieves against this noise: pink noise and a low rumble, which is where feed-forward cancellation is strongest, with a reference microphone that hears the same thing the ear does. A real headset also faces wind, a moving fit, a secondary path that has to be measured rather than assumed, and noise that is not so obliging. Treat the number as the behaviour of the algorithm, not as a promise about the built headset.

How to read the simulation The signals are synthetic, not recordings. The trained GTCRN network cannot run in a browser, so a classical Wiener mask stands in for block B7 — the real one runs on the Jetson through TensorRT. The frame time in the readout is this browser's, not the Jetson's. Block B10, the residual LMS, is an optional stage and is drawn faded because it is bypassed in this build.
