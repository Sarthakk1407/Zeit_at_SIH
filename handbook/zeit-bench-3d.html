What this model shows

This is the prototype as it is specified to be built — not an illustration of a concept. The headset, the audio interface and the single-board computer are modelled to their published dimensions, connected by the cabling they actually require, and the signal is traced through them in the order it physically travels.

The geometry is constructed procedurally in code rather than loaded from downloaded assets. That is partly a constraint — this page is served under a policy that blocks external 3D files — and partly a decision: every dimension comes from a datasheet or from docs/01-system/architecture.md, so what is on screen is the specification rather than an artist's approximation, and there is no third-party asset licence to carry into a defence deliverable.

Why the bench is laid out this way

Everything sits on one plane. The communication unit is laid flat rather than stood upright, the power supply is set back behind the board, and the headset stand is cut to the height of the head it carries. A bench where one component towers over the others reads as a pile of parts; a bench where nothing is taller than it needs to be reads as a system.

The surface is divided into three marked zones that correspond exactly to the three stages of Lane B — capture, compute, sink. Lane A appears in none of them, because Lane A never leaves the earcup.

The two lanes, shown separately

The most common mistake on this problem statement is treating "noise cancellation" as one thing. It is two systems, with different physics, different budgets and different metrics, sharing three microphones. The lane filter in the controls removes one so the other can be read on its own.

Sound crosses from the outer earcup shell to the eardrum in roughly 0.3–0.6 ms. A feedforward canceller has to get its anti-noise there first. Lane B's budget is forty to eighty times too slow for that, which is why the two cannot be fused into one chain no matter how good the model is — and why the Lane A module is separate hardware that keeps protecting hearing even when the speech engine is not running.

The earcup is drawn cut away

The right earcup's outer shell is modelled as an arc rather than a closed cylinder, so the driver, the error microphone and the Lane A module are visible without switching to the exploded view. The four green legs are the complete loop: reference in from Mic 2, anti-noise out to the driver, the acoustic leg to the ear, and the error return from Mic 3.

One correction is worth stating in place, because an earlier revision of the architecture had it wrong: Mic 3 does not feed the transmit chain. It measures what the wearer hears, and the transmitted voice never passes through the wearer's ear. Select the Lane B filter and Mic 3 disappears from the scene, which is exactly right. Lane B's optional residual stage is driven by Mic 2 — Widrow's original two-input canceller.

No development computer in the signal path

The deployed chain is microphones → audio interface → single-board computer → communication unit, with a return path to the earcups and Lane A closing its own loop inside the cup. The board boots from its own card, runs the engine headlessly and never needs a host.

The micro-HDMI cable is retained on the parts list because the board has to be configured at some point, but it is bring-up equipment and is deliberately not drawn on the bench.

The scenario the run plays

A shooter at 30 m firing a supersonic round. Every timing is derived from that geometry and the speed of sound, not chosen for effect.

Reading the signal monitor

Four traces, one per lane role. MIC 1 carries the voice with the crack and blast on top of it. MIC 2 carries the same impulsive events with no voice — that separation is what Widrow's two-input architecture depends on, and it is a mechanical requirement rather than a software one. MIC 3 is Lane A's result: what is left at the ear after the cup's passive attenuation and the anti-noise together. OUT is Lane B's result, what reaches the radio.

Watch the moment the blast lands. On MIC 1 the waveform saturates and the voice is buried. On MIC 3 the residual collapses within a millisecond — that is the whole of Lane A. On OUT the impulse is suppressed and the speech envelope resumes over tens of milliseconds, and that interval is marked as IRT, the impulse recovery time. IRT is the number that matters operationally and the one PESQ and STOI average away: a gunshot destroys roughly 200 ms, and across a ten-second clip the average barely moves.

The monitor traces are generated from the physical model described above and are illustrative of the intended behaviour. Neither lane has been built, so no trace here is a measured result, and the −18.4 dB residual is a design target rather than an observation. Every figure that is measured — and every figure that is not — is identified in the System Handbook.
