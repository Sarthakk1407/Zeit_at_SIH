What this model shows

This is the prototype as it is specified to be built — not an illustration of a concept. The headset, the audio interface and the single-board computer are modelled to their published dimensions, connected by the cabling they actually require, and the signal is traced through them in the order it physically travels.

The geometry is constructed procedurally in code rather than loaded from downloaded assets. That is partly a constraint — this page is served under a policy that blocks external 3D files — and partly a decision: every dimension comes from a datasheet or from docs/01-system/architecture.md, so what is on screen is the specification rather than an artist's approximation, and there is no third-party asset licence to carry into a defence deliverable.

No development computer in the signal path

The deployed chain is microphones → audio interface → single-board computer → communication unit, with a return path to the earcups. The board boots from its own card, runs the engine headlessly and never needs a host.

The micro-HDMI cable is retained on the parts list because the board has to be configured at some point, but it is bring-up equipment and is deliberately not drawn on the bench. The problem statement calls for a prototype integrated with microphones and headphones or communication units, and that is what is modelled.

The scenario the run plays

A shooter at 30 m firing a supersonic round. Every timing is derived from that geometry and the speed of sound, not chosen for effect.

0 msTriggerThe muzzle blast begins expanding at 343 m/s
36.5 msBullet passesAt roughly Mach 2.4 the round reaches its closest approach and the shockwave is generated there — about 3 m away, not 30

+0.58 msMic 1 after Mic 2The two microphones are about 20 cm apart on one head. Far too small to use as prediction, which is why the architecture detects and reacts rather than forecasting

Reading the signal monitor

The three traces are the heart of the demonstration. MIC 1 carries the voice with the crack and blast on top of it. MIC 2 carries the same impulsive events with no voice — that separation is what Widrow's two-input architecture depends on, and it is a mechanical requirement rather than a software one. ENGINE OUT is what reaches the radio.

Watch the moment the blast lands. On MIC 1 the waveform saturates and the voice is buried. On ENGINE OUT the impulse is suppressed and the speech envelope resumes — and the interval before it fully recovers is marked as IRT, the impulse recovery time. That interval is the number that matters operationally and the one PESQ and STOI average away: a gunshot destroys roughly 200 ms, and across a ten-second clip the average barely moves.

The monitor traces are generated from the physical model described above and are illustrative of the intended behaviour. The engine has not been built, so no trace here is a measured result. Every figure that is measured — and every figure that is not — is identified in the System Handbook.
