The gaps we build on

Each one is a place a published method stops short of what the PS demands. Every claim is checkable against a specific section or table — verify before it goes on a slide.

11.1 — There is no impulsive noise anywhere in Deep ANC

Its test noises are engine, factory, babble and speech-shaped noise from NOISEX-92, in every result table. All four are stationary or close to it. Engine noise hums, factory noise grinds, babble is a steady wash, and SSN is literally noise shaped like the average speech spectrum. Not one gunshot, not one door slam, nothing with a sharp attack. Training used a sound-effects library that probably contained transients, but nothing was ever tested on them.

The foundational deep-ANC paper does not address the noise class our sponsor names first.

What we do: run the test the authors never ran, and report the collapse honestly.

11.2 — Delay compensation quietly assumes the noise is predictable

Deep ANC buys back latency by predicting the anti-noise M frames ahead. With 20 ms frames and a 10 ms hop, each frame of prediction buys 10 ms. Its cost on engine noise: −11.07 dB with no prediction, −9.60 dB at one frame, −7.93 dB at two. Roughly 1.5–1.7 dB lost per 10 ms bought back — on periodic noise.

Prediction only works if the future resembles the past. A gunshot is by definition unpredictable. So the mechanism that makes deep ANC real-time-viable should degrade far worse on impulsive noise than 1.5 dB per frame — possibly to nothing. Nobody has measured this.

What we do: replicate that table with impulsive noise substituted in, and plot both curves on the same axes. And it dictates the architecture: if you cannot predict a gunshot, you must detect and react instead.

11.3 — RMS normalisation is formally invalid for impulsive noise

The dual-mic paper rescales every mixture so the waveform RMS equals 1. Its authors are honest that this is not causal and that deployment would need causal AGC — but RMS is a second-moment statistic, and for α-stable noise with α < 2 the variance is infinite. You cannot normalise heavy-tailed audio by RMS in any principled way. The preprocessing breaks before the model runs.

What we do: percentile scaling first, then FLOM, then causal AGC. Half a day of work; a real defect in the closest prior system; explainable in thirty seconds.

11.4 — Microphone-side clipping is modelled by nobody

Deep ANC carefully models loudspeaker nonlinearity with a scaled error function at four levels, trained and tested. Good work, all of it on the output side. Nothing models what happens when the input overloads — even though a 140 dB blast arrives at the converter already flat-topped.

What we do: extend their nonlinearity framework to the input side, same spirit and opposite end of the chain, and demonstrate with deliberately clipped recordings we made ourselves. validate.py records precisely which events clipped and by how much.

11.5 — The α-stable clipping constraint, inverted

At α below 0.5, roughly a third of the α-stable paper's image pixel values ended up clipped, which they judged to compromise reliability — so they set 0.5 as a lower bound. For them clipping was an artifact of squeezing unbounded noise into a bounded pixel range: a nuisance to design around. For us clipping is the physics. They also note that truncated α-stable noise still beat truncated Gaussian, so the effect survives clipping; it only complicated their analysis.

What we do: generate below their floor deliberately, let it clip, and train the model to reconstruct through it. We use the regime they excluded, for the reason they excluded it — and can point at their sentence to explain why the constraint does not apply to us.

11.6 — α-stable augmentation has never touched audio

The authors say so in their conclusion: noise substantially affects audio, video and text, and extension is future work. Their four datasets are two image sets and two time-series sets. Not one audio experiment. And everything is classification; enhancement is regression, which they also list as unexplored.

What we do: be first — but do not overclaim. Frame it as a hypothesis with a Gaussian-augmented baseline alongside. If it helps, we extended a published method to a new task class. If it does not, we have an honest negative result with an explanation. Assuming it works and quietly not checking is the failure mode.

11.7 — α-stable training produces sparser models, and nobody pruned them

Reported as a side effect: 6.16 % and 7.13 % sparsity versus 3.02 % for Gaussian on MNIST-C; 59.49 % versus 51.62 % on CIFAR10-C. The authors connect it to generalisation, cite the pruning literature, and never prune anything. The dual-mic paper spends a whole section on structured pruning. The two papers do not cite each other, and the untested hypothesis sits in the gap.

What we do: one experiment, two deliverables.

11.9 — The three cross-paper findings

Visible only when all the papers are read together. None of them cites the others in the relevant way.

A. Deep ANC's latency trick and the α-stable noise model are in direct conflict. Deep ANC buys latency back by prediction; prediction needs predictability. The α-stable premise is that impulsive noise is heavy-tailed — dominated by rare, extreme, isolated events, which are by construction the least predictable thing in a signal. The mechanism that makes deep ANC real-time-viable should fail exactly on the noise class the PS is about. Measured, this is the intellectual spine of the project.

B. The dual-mic preprocessing is mathematically undefined under the α-stable noise model. RMS is a second moment; for α < 2 the variance is infinite. The closest published dual-mic system has a preprocessing step that is not merely fragile but formally invalid for our noise class — and the fix is cited inside the other paper.

C. Both enhancement papers use squared-error losses, the wrong estimator here. Squared error is maximum-likelihood when the residual is Gaussian. Under heavy tails it is dominated by outliers — one impulsive sample drags the gradient more than a second of speech. L1 is better, still not matched. A loss built on fractional lower-order moments would be principled, and is the stretch goal.

Two cautions carried from the gap analysis

Verify before you cite. Sections and tables are pointed to rather than reproduced. Open each one and confirm before it goes near a slide. If a number in the notes disagrees with the paper, the paper is right.

A gap in the literature is not automatically a good project. Sometimes nobody did a thing because it does not work, or because it does not matter. Gaps 11.2, 11.3, 11.5 and 11.7 are defensible as genuinely valuable. The rest are worth checking before committing weeks.
