The offline pipeline

What plays the role of a backend. There is no server and no API — there is a workstation pipeline that produces one file, and that file is the only thing that ships.

"Where is your backend?" is a question that will be asked, and the answer is worth having ready: the backend is the offline pipeline, and its output is model.onnx. Everything from data generation to quantisation lives here. None of it runs on the device.

Stage 1 — Real data acquisition

Covered in full in section 06. One trip, 96 kHz / 24-bit, calibrated to absolute Pascals, with the range impulse response captured before anything else that can be shortened.

Stage 2 — The synthetic generator

The real recordings are the reference; the generator produces the training material. The chain, in order — and most naïve implementations stop at step 2, which is why they sound synthetic:

  1. Sample source parameters — peak overpressure Ps, positive-phase duration T₀, decay coefficient b — from a distribution fitted to the measured fit set.
  2. Generate a Friedlander waveform: p(t) = Ps·(1 − t/T₀)·e^(−b·t/T₀) — instantaneous rise, exponential decay, then a negative underpressure phase.
  3. Apply directivity by azimuth. A blast is not a point source; it is much louder in front of the weapon than behind it.
  4. Apply spherical spreading over distance — 6 dB per doubling.
  5. Apply atmospheric absorption, which depends strongly on temperature and humidity, and which is why those fields are logged.
  6. Add the ground reflection — delay from the logged geometry, coefficient from the ground surface. This is what produces the comb filtering visible in the spectrum.
  7. Convolve with the measured range impulse response, or an outdoor propagation model.
  8. Layer the mechanical action sounds recorded separately at close range. Almost nobody records this layer, and it is what makes a synthetic shot sound like a weapon rather than a bare pressure pulse.
  9. Pass through the device chain — clipping, AGC, converter quantisation, and the vocoder.

Published anchors worth checking your output against: a .22 pistol is roughly 146 dB at 1 m with a peak near 750 Hz; a .357 Magnum around 155 dB with a peak near 400 Hz. Peak frequency shifts down as calibre grows.

The device chain, modelled explicitly

Firearms exceed 154 dB at 3 m; consumer microphones saturate around 120–130 dB. The field microphone will clip. So model it rather than pretending otherwise:

  • Hard and soft clipping at configurable thresholds, calibrated against the deliberately-clipped range recordings.
  • AGC with attack and release, so the pumping behaviour is represented.
  • Microphone self-noise floor and converter quantisation.
  • A low-bitrate vocoder (MELPe or AMBE class) simulating the radio link downstream of the denoiser.

Calibrating the clipping model against real clipped recordings is what makes it defensible rather than guessed — and it is the direct answer to the gap where every published paper models loudspeaker saturation on the output side and nobody models microphone saturation on the input side.

Validating the generator

Measure the synthetic output with the same engine that measured the real audio, and compare against the holdout split only. session.py verify confirms the engine has not changed since the reference was frozen.

The comparison happens on features.json, not on audio. Audio is the source; two JSON files are what get compared.

Stage 3 — Mixing and augmentation

Clean speech convolved with a spread of room responses, mixed with noise at controlled SNRs, then augmented. Everything generated on the fly at train time from a seeded config — no pre-rendered mixture files. That keeps the repository small, makes every experiment reproducible from a config hash, and allows SNR and room response to be resampled every epoch.

Keep civilian noise in the mix. A model trained only on gunshots fails on everything else. Target roughly 35 % impulsive, 35 % non-stationary, 30 % stationary.

For outdoor gunshot propagation, do not use room impulse responses. Model the direct path, the ground reflection and atmospheric absorption analytically instead — a room RIR encodes a small enclosure and will make an open field sound like a corridor.

The largest dataset gap in the project — audit F8

Under 80+ dB of noise, people involuntarily change how they speak: higher pitch, more level, shifted spectral tilt. That is the Lombard effect, and systems trained on neutral read speech lose roughly 5 dB when tested on Lombard speech — a gap that persists even after level normalisation.

The entire use case here is a person shouting under fire. Aim for at least 20 % Lombard content in the training mix. Lombard GRID recorded each of its 54 speakers in both conditions, with the Lombard condition induced by 80 dB SPL speech-shaped noise over headphones.

Also worth doing: boom-mic emulation. Clean corpora are recorded at about 30 cm; a headset boom microphone sits at 2–5 cm. Apply a low-shelf boost for the proximity effect, light saturation, and occasional plosive bursts before mixing, or the model learns the wrong voice.

The augmentation — where the novelty is

  1. α-stable noise instead of Gaussian, α ≈ 1. The α parameter controls tail heaviness: α = 2 is Gaussian, α = 1 is Cauchy, smaller means more violent spikes. Justified by the Generalized Central Limit Theorem. Using several separate α values beats blending them into one mixture.
  2. Below the α = 0.5 floor, and let it clip. The α-stable authors excluded α < 0.5 because roughly a third of their image pixel values ended up clipped, which they judged to compromise reliability. For us clipping is the physics — a microphone has a maximum SPL and a gunshot exceeds it every single time. We use the regime they excluded, for the reason they excluded it. They also note that truncated α-stable noise still beat truncated Gaussian, so the effect survives clipping.
  3. Input-side saturation, mirroring Deep ANC's scaled-error-function loudspeaker model at the opposite end of the chain.
  4. Structured bursts, not i.i.d. spikes. Their noise is independent sample by sample; a real gunshot has structure over tens of milliseconds — sharp attack, specific spectral shape, exponential decay, reflection tail. Generate heavy-tailed arrival and amplitude but with a measured envelope.
  5. γ derived from target SNR rather than swept blindly. Choosing the dispersion parameter is an open problem in images because there is no natural scale; in audio there is one, and it is SNR.
  6. A wide spread of room responses, near-anechoic through hard-walled, reported separately rather than averaged.
  7. HRTF-based head shadow instead of a flat scalar. The closest prior work scales the secondary channel by a random −10 to 0 dB; real head shadow is strongly frequency-dependent, and their own figure shows it varying across frequency.

Stage 5 — Compression and export

  • Structured pruning. Remove whole groups of weights — a full convolution kernel, a whole column of a recurrent matrix — rather than scattered individual ones, because hardware can actually skip a group while scattered zeros still get multiplied. Use sparse group lasso with per-tensor sensitivity analysis, then prune and fine-tune iteratively. Reference result: 290.44 K → 103.07 K parameters over six iterations, with STOI down 0.72 %, PESQ down 0.05 and SNR down 0.53 dB at 0 dB input.
  • Quantisation. The study the reference work never did. INT8, both post-training and quantisation-aware. Report the interaction explicitly: prune-then-quantise is not the same as quantise-then-prune, and speech enhancement is sensitive to reduced precision because it manipulates fine spectral detail.
  • The bonus experiment. α-stable training produces measurably sparser models — 6.16 % and 7.13 % versus 3.02 % on MNIST-C, 59.49 % versus 51.62 % on CIFAR10-C — and nobody has pruned one. Train two identical models, one Gaussian-augmented and one α-stable, prune both with the same scheme, and plot quality against parameter count. One experiment, two deliverables: better impulsive robustness and better edge compression from a single training change.
  • Export. PyTorch → torch.onnx.export with a pinned opset → ONNX Runtime on ARM, or TensorRT if the board turns out to be a Jetson, or TFLite Micro / CMSIS-NN for the microcontroller argument.

Hand-written rather than taken from a library, because no library does exactly these: the lock-free ring buffer, Aux-IVA, robust normalisation over a sliding window, the BMRI impulsive path, the transient classifier, the LMS residual, and the spectral-correction guard.

The silent failure that costs a week

The C++ implementation produces slightly different numbers from the Python one it was trained as — a different FFT scaling, a different window, a mismatched ERB band edge, a normalisation constant off by one. Nothing crashes. The model just quietly performs worse than it did in training, and you spend a week blaming the model.

The defence is a numerical parity test: fix a small set of input WAVs, run the full Python pipeline dumping every intermediate — after normalisation, after STFT, after the model, after ISTFT — run the C++ pipeline on the same inputs dumping the same points, and assert agreement to a stated tolerance at every stage. Stage-by-stage matters: if only the final output is compared, a normalisation error and an inverse-STFT error can cancel out on the test file and diverge everywhere else. Build it before the C++ path is finished, not after.

The C++ build order

Deliberately arranged so the riskiest question — does the deadline hold on the real board at all — is answered first, and so that steps 1 to 4 need no trained model and no range data.

  1. Skeleton: audio in → ring buffer → audio out, passthrough. Proves I/O, buffer sizes and the real deadline before any DSP exists.
  2. rt_safety_test — catch allocations in the callback while the code is still small.
  3. STFT → ISTFT, perfect reconstruction. If this is not bit-accurate, nothing after it can be trusted.
  4. The parity harness with just the STFT stage in it, to establish the pattern before there is much to compare.
  5. Robust normalisation, both sides. The one fix that must exist before any measurement means anything.
  6. ONNX Runtime with a dummy model — proves the export path and measures inference cost with a placeholder.
  7. The BMRI path in C++, which is independent of the model and can be built in parallel with training.
  8. Real model, full parity, benchmark. Everything meets here.

Three numbers to measure on the actual board

  • Milliseconds per frame, per block, worst case over a long run — not the mean.
  • MMAC/s, counted from the model graph and cross-checked against measured time.
  • Watts, from a USB power meter, idle versus running. Nobody in the literature reports this.
