01

The problem, precisely

What PS 26052 asks for, what each requirement rules out, and the three physical facts that decide the whole design.

A soldier speaks into a headset while a gun fires nearby. The person at the other end of the radio should hear the voice, clearly, and not the gunshot. That is the entire job.

The five requirements, and what each one closes off

RequirementWhat it rules out
AI/ML-enabledA purely classical DSP solution. The core denoiser must be learned, not hand-designed
AdaptiveFixed settings. The system must change behaviour as the noise changes — not just be tuned once
Stationary + non-stationary + impulsiveAny method validated only on steady noise — which is nearly all of the published literature
High speech intelligibilityAggressive suppression that eats the voice. Measured, not asserted
Real-time on embedded hardwareLarge models. Latency and power become first-class constraints, not afterthoughts

The stated targets are SNR > 15 dB, STOI > 0.85, PESQ > 2.5, real-time, low latency. Section 09 explains why those three numbers must be reported as curves rather than as a single pass/fail gate.

The third row is the one that matters. Gunshots and artillery are named in the first paragraph of the problem statement, and — as section 11 shows — almost nothing in the published literature is tested on them.

The three facts that decide the architecture

  1. The gunshot is enormously louder than the voice. A muzzle blast is 140–155 dB SPL; a shouted voice is 80–90 dB. That is a factor of ten thousand or more in pressure. Any processing that assumes the two are comparable in size is wrong from the first line.
  2. It arrives with no warning. There is nothing in the milliseconds before a gunshot that says one is coming. This single fact kills every technique that works by prediction, which is the mechanism the published state of the art relies on to stay real-time.
  3. It has to run in real time on a battery. No cloud, no laptop, no waiting. That makes SWaP-C — size, weight, power and cost — the binding constraint, not model quality.
The intellectual spine of the project

The published state of the art buys back processing delay by predicting noise one or two frames ahead. That works for engine hum, which repeats. It cannot work for a gunshot, which is by definition unpredictable. So this architecture does not predict — it detects and reacts. Measuring how badly prediction collapses on impulsive noise, when nobody has ever measured it, is the single result the whole project argues from.

Stated honestly, because the gap between what is built and what is designed is the most important fact about this project right now.

Two things exist: a field-measurement toolkit that works, and a design that is unusually well argued. Nothing of the product itself has been written yet.

That is the correct order for a project whose hardest input — real, calibrated gunshot recordings — can only be obtained once, on a single trip, with no retry. The seven-step plan

  1. Measure real impulsive noise ourselves, calibrated to absolute pressure, on one range trip — so we know what we are modelling rather than guessing from downloaded clips.
  2. Build a synthetic generator producing unlimited realistic gunshots, and validate it against the real recordings we deliberately held back.
  3. Mix that noise with clean speech at controlled signal-to-noise ratios to make training pairs — a noisy input and the clean answer.
  4. Train a very small neural network — GTCRN, about 24,000 parameters — to strip the noise out.
  5. Put a fast classical path beside it for sharp transient events a network handles badly, with a live classifier deciding which path leads.
  6. Shrink the model by pruning and quantisation, export to ONNX, run it in C++ on a small board.
  7. Measure everything on the real board — latency, compute, and watts, which nobody in the literature reports.
03

Two lanes, not one system

The most common mistake on this problem statement is treating "noise cancellation" as a single thing. It is two systems with different physics, budgets and metrics.

Everything the problem statement measures — SNR, STOI, PESQ — is Lane B. Everything the phrase "active noise cancellation" strictly means is Lane A. The PS asks for both, so the system must contain both, and every number in the pitch must say which lane it belongs to.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Sound crosses from the outer earcup shell to the eardrum in roughly 0.3–0.6 ms. A feedforward canceller has to get its anti-noise there first. Lane B's budget is 25.3 ms — forty to eighty times too slow. If the two lanes are fused into one chain, the resulting system cannot do active cancellation at all, no matter how good the model is. Keeping them separate is not a stylistic choice; it is the difference between a system that works and one that cannot.

The two lanes are also complementary in what they are good at. Lane A, being classical adaptive filtering, is genuinely strong on low-frequency continuous noise — engine, rotor, generator, vehicle rumble. Lane B, being learned, is strong on broadband, non-stationary and impulsive content in the speech channel. Saying so explicitly is worth doing: it explains why both exist rather than one being a weaker version of the other.

04

The architecture, layer by layer

Six layers, from air pressure to a checksummed dataset. Confusing which layer something belongs to is the most common mistake in this project.

L0

Acoustic layer — air, geometry, physics

Where the sound is before anything electrical touches it. Muzzle blast, ballistic crack, ground reflection, head shadow, distance, air absorption. Nothing downstream can recover what is lost here.

Owned by: physics · Modelled by: the synthetic generator
L1

Transduction layer — microphones and conversion

Three microphones, preamplifier gain, and the analogue-to-digital converter. This is where clipping happens, and clipping is irreversible.

Owned by: hardware · Constraint: mic max SPL vs a 140–155 dB blast
L2

Lane A — real-time hearing protection

A tight feedback loop on a DSP or microcontroller. Reference mic in, anti-noise out, in-ear mic as the error signal. Never touches the transmitted voice.

Owned by: hardware/DSP · Budget: under 1 ms
L3

Lane B — the eleven-block speech engine

The C++ real-time path. Normalise, transform, classify, split into two paths, recombine, protect, reconstruct. The only place a neural network runs.

Owned by: software · Budget: 10 ms of compute per 10 ms hop
L4

Offline layer — what plays the role of a backend

Data generation, mixing, augmentation, training, pruning, quantisation, evaluation. Runs on a workstation. None of it ships; it only shapes the weights that do.

Owned by: software · Budget: unlimited
L5

Provenance layer — how we prove any of it

Calibration to absolute pressure, fit/holdout split assigned before data exists, SHA-256 of data and of the measurement code, config-hash reproducibility.

Owned by: everyone · This is what a DRDO evaluator actually audits
The distinction judges test

α-stable augmentation, the synthetic generator, pruning, every metric — none of it ships. It shapes the weights that ship. A judge asking "isn't α-stable noise generation too heavy for embedded?" has misunderstood the question, and you should be able to say why in one sentence: it runs once, on a workstation, months before the device is switched on.

L0 — What a gunshot actually is

Not one sound but four, arriving at different times:

  • The ballistic crack, if the round is supersonic. The bullet drags a cone-shaped shockwave behind it. Because the bullet outruns sound, this always arrives before the muzzle blast. It is the physics counter-sniper systems are built on, and our only genuine source of look-ahead.
  • The muzzle blast — the expanding pressure wave from propellant gas leaving the barrel. 140–155 dB at close range, with a rise time of 10–50 microseconds. Idealised by the Friedlander wave.
  • The mechanical layer — bolt cycling, brass ejection, trigger. 55–85 dB, buried by the blast in any single recording, which is why the plan records it separately at close range and high gain.
  • The environment's answer — ground reflection, which produces visible comb filtering in the spectrum, and reverberation decaying over tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

The whole event spans roughly 100 dB of dynamic range:

The hardware rule software cannot fix

Mic 2 must not hear the voice. An adaptive filter works by making total output power as small as possible. If the voice appears in the reference microphone, the mathematically optimal action is to remove the voice. Widrow proved this in 1975. It is a mechanical requirement — a physical barrier between the mouth and Mic 2, which is why the boom and Mic 2 sit on opposite sides of the head with the earcup shell between them — and no amount of software repairs it afterwards.

Why clipping is decided here and nowhere else

An ordinary microphone capsule saturates somewhere above 110–120 dB SPL; a 3.5 mm electret tops out around 100–120 dB. A muzzle blast at 1 m is 140–155 dB. The waveform therefore arrives at the converter already flat-topped, with information physically destroyed. No pad, no gain change, no algorithm downstream recovers it. Distance is the only real control — every doubling buys 6 dB.

This is why the data pipeline deliberately models input-side saturation. Every published paper models the loudspeaker saturating on the output side; nobody models the microphone saturating on the input side, even though that is what actually happens on a range.

Practical consequence for the build: use a dynamic (moving-coil) microphone for close blast work — it has no active electronics to overload — and a measurement microphone for sweeps, calibration and ambience.

Threading — how the work divides across time

The hard rule: everything must finish within 10 ms per hop.

20 ms frame with a 10 ms hop was chosen for three reasons: it matches the frame size used by the closest prior dual-microphone work, so latency and compute comparisons are like-for-like; 25.3 ms sits inside what radio communication tolerates; and 10 ms of compute is comfortable, since that prior work needed 2.78 ms per 20 ms frame on a laptop CPU and a GTCRN-class model is far smaller.

An unresolved conflict in this table — audit F6

GTCRN and H-GTCRN as published use a 32 ms window with a 16 ms hop at 16 kHz, mapping 192 high-frequency bins to 64 ERB bands while keeping 65 low-frequency bins intact. That arithmetic assumes 257 bins from a 512-point FFT at 16 kHz. Choosing 20/10 ms at 48 kHz means the published band mapping, the 23.7 K parameter count and the 39.6 MMAC/s figure all stop applying and the model must be retrained. At GTCRN's own settings the real algorithmic latency is closer to 48 ms than 25.3 ms. Either choice is defensible; claiming both is not. The rate chain — 96 kHz capture, 48 kHz engine, 16 kHz neural core, and the anti-alias filters between them — needs to be written down and its latency counted.

01

Audio input

real-time thread · ~0 compute

Reads 128 samples at a time from three microphones and pushes them into a lock-free ring buffer. It does almost nothing, on purpose.

Why it does nothing: this runs on the audio callback thread, which has a hard deadline. If it is ever late, the sound card runs out of samples and the listener hears a click. So it copies and hands off. It never allocates memory, never takes a lock, never logs, never throws an exception, never grows a std::vector and never touches a std::string. Any one of those can block for an unpredictable time, and violating the rule produces a click that appears once every few minutes and is almost impossible to debug afterwards.

02

Aux-IVA — blind source separation

~0.2 MMAC/s per iteration · negligible params

Auxiliary-function-based Independent Vector Analysis. Classical, no training, no neural network.

Given three mixed recordings it pulls them apart into three separate streams without being told anything about the sources. It works by assuming the underlying sources are statistically independent and finding the un-mixing that maximises that independence — and, unlike plain ICA, it keeps each source's frequency bins tied together so one source does not get scattered across several outputs.

Why the auxiliary-function variant: ordinary IVA needs a hand-tuned step size. Too small and it converges too slowly for real time; too large and it becomes unstable. The auxiliary-function form has no step size at all and is mathematically guaranteed to improve at every iteration. That is what makes it usable on a device.

What it honestly gives you: three separated streams in unknown order (permutation ambiguity) and unknown scale (scaling ambiguity). It does not label them. That is fine, because we feed all of them to the network and let it decide — which is exactly what H-GTCRN's ablation found works best.

Scope honesty — audit F9

H-GTCRN is dual-channel, with two microphones 4 cm apart — not three. The three-microphone design is our extension. And IVA is a determined method: N microphones separate at most N sources. A real scene has voice, blast, engine, wind and babble, which is underdetermined, so the three outputs are each still mixtures. Say "three separated streams, unordered" rather than labelling them voice / impulsive / background on a diagram.

03

Robust normalisation — the fix that must come first

negligible · must be allocation-free

Audio must be scaled to a standard loudness before a model sees it, otherwise the model has to learn to handle every possible volume. Everyone does this with RMS — average energy. RMS is the wrong tool here, and not slightly wrong. It is formally undefined.

The practical problem: a gunshot can be 40 dB above the voice. RMS is dominated by the loudest samples, so the gunshot sets the scale. Divide by it and the voice is squashed toward the numeric noise floor. Worse, the scale factor changes depending on whether a gunshot happened to land inside the window or not.

The formal problem: impulsive noise is heavy-tailed. For α-stable distributions with α below 2 the variance is infinite. RMS is built on variance. So it is not merely fragile here — it does not exist.

What we use instead, in order of ambition:

  1. 90th-percentile scaling — take the level 90 % of samples fall below and scale by that, so one spike cannot move it. Cheap, works, do this first.
  2. FLOM — fractional lower-order moments (Shao & Nikias, 1993), statistics built on a power below 2, which stay finite when variance does not. The principled version.
  3. Causal AGC with a fast-attack limiter — solves the deployment problem and the impulsive problem together.
Highest return on effort in the project

The closest published dual-microphone system rescales every mixture so the waveform RMS equals 1. Its authors are honest that this is not causal and that deployment would need automatic gain control — but they do not notice that the statistic itself is undefined for their target noise class. This is a real defect in the nearest prior work, the fix is cited inside another paper we already hold, it takes about half a day, and it is explainable in thirty seconds. Everything measured downstream is meaningless until it is done.

04

STFT + ERB — turning sound into a picture

FFT dominates the budget · PFFFT

STFT — the Short-Time Fourier Transform. Chop the audio into short overlapping slices called frames, and for each slice work out how much energy sits at each frequency. The result is a picture with time across and frequency up: a spectrogram. Speech looks like stacked horizontal stripes; a gunshot looks like a vertical wall.

We keep the complex form — both magnitude and phase. Phase matters for reconstruction quality; discard it and the output sounds smeared and metallic. One caveat worth carrying: H-GTCRN's own ablation found a magnitude-only log-power feature beat the full complex feature on its separation branch, concluding that phase information "is not as crucial". So "phase is always essential" is too strong a claim to make on a slide.

ERB — Equivalent Rectangular Bandwidth. Instead of hundreds of equal-width frequency bins, group them into about 32 bands that get wider as frequency rises, the way the human ear does. Fewer numbers to process, and the ones kept are the ones people can actually hear. This is where most of the compute saving comes from.

GTCRN is specific about it: 192 high-frequency bands merged to 64 ERB bands, 65 low-frequency bands left untouched, giving a 129-dimensional compressed feature map. The low bands are preserved because that is where speech harmonics live.

05

The classifier — this is the "adaptive" the PS demands

tiny · a few hundred operations

A small, fast piece of code that looks at each frame and asks: is this steady noise, changing noise, or a sudden bang?

It measures kurtosis — a number describing how spiky a signal is. Steady Gaussian noise sits at 3. The Cauchy noise BMRI was validated on measures 27. A gunshot is orders of magnitude higher. Based on the answer it sets two dials: γ (gamma), how aggressively to clean, and β (beta), the detection threshold.

Why this block is load-bearing: the problem statement asks for an adaptive system. This is the piece that makes it adaptive. Without it the pipeline behaves identically for a gunshot and for engine hum, and the word "adaptive" becomes an assertion. The BMRI paper tunes γ by hand, offline, from a chart; replacing that with a live classifier is genuinely our change.

It gives operator modes for free, because γ has a known relationship to how much gets removed:

06

Impulsive path — BMRI

2048-sample blocks · AR order 16–32 · single pass

Binary Mask Residual Interpolation (Ruhland, Bitzer, Brandt & Goetze, 2015). Classical, fast, no neural component. Two steps:

1 · Split. A frequency-dependent adaptive threshold — reacting slowly when sound rises and quickly when it falls — separates each block into a "target" part that is probably signal and a "residual" part that is probably noise.

2 · Detect and fill. An AR model — autoregressive, predicting each sample from the handful before it — runs over the residual. Where the actual sample diverges wildly from the prediction, that sample is an impulse. The worst γ % are thrown away and replaced by interpolation from their neighbours. A final spectral-correction pass removes the low-frequency rumble the interpolation reintroduces.

Why this path exists at all. Deep ANC reduces its own latency by predicting noise one or two frames ahead. That works for engine hum, which repeats. It cannot work for a gunshot. So this path does not predict — it waits, detects and reacts. That is the single most important architectural decision in the system, and it follows directly from the physics rather than from taste.

Its cost is small by design: 2048-sample blocks, AR order 16 or 32, one interpolation pass, no learned component. Those constants come straight from the paper and should be benchmarked against the latency budget rather than assumed.

Scope limit — audit F4

BMRI was validated on white Cauchy noise at density 0.02, kurtosis 27, restoring music and speech — optical soundtrack noise on celluloid, rain, gramophone clicks. Not gunshots. Its authors explicitly designed it to avoid artefacts "be it at the cost of obtaining less noise reduction than possible", which is the opposite of our goal. Its SNR improvement is around 3.5 dB at the matched setting, against a PS target of 15 dB. And it is known to degrade transients: the paper reports negative improvement on pop music because "transients like e.g. drum sounds cause high AR error signals that trigger the interpolation threshold and thus get degraded". Speech plosives are transients.

Use it where it fits: the ballistic crack (sub-millisecond) and the mechanical layer. For a 50–200 ms muzzle blast, interpolation from neighbours is not interpolation, it is synthesis, and an AR model of order 16–32 cannot do it. The honest goal there is conceal and recover, measured by Impulse Recovery Time.

07

Neural core — GTCRN

23.7 K params · 39.6 MMAC/s (paper) · 48.2 K / 33.0 (official code)

Grouped Temporal Convolutional Recurrent Network (Rong et al., ICASSP 2024). This is the AI/ML the problem statement requires, and the only learned block in the chain.

It takes the noisy spectrogram and produces a mask — a set of numbers, one per frequency band per time slice, saying "keep this much of this band". Multiply the noisy spectrum by the mask and the noise is gone. Specifically it predicts a complex ratio mask, so it corrects phase as well as magnitude, with a tanh output bounding the values to ±1.

Five tricks make it small enough for a headset:

  • ERB band merging — 192 high-frequency bins compressed to 64 perceptual bands, 65 low bins kept intact. Detail preserved where harmonics live.
  • Grouped convolution — split channels into groups processed independently, then shuffled so information still crosses. Far fewer connections for nearly the same capability.
  • Grouped RNN — the same idea applied to the memory part. Input features and hidden states split into 2 groups, each fed to a recurrent layer with half the parameters.
  • SFE, subband feature extraction — unfold each frequency band together with its k−1 neighbours and stack them into the channel dimension, so the following convolution can use frequency relationships directly.
  • TRA, temporal recurrent attention — pool energy across frequency to get a per-frame energy curve, run it through a small GRU and a fully-connected layer, and use the sigmoid output to weight moments in time. It beats plain time attention for almost no extra compute.

Structurally it is an encoder–decoder: two convolution blocks and three grouped temporal convolution blocks down, a grouped dual-path RNN in the middle (bidirectional GRU across frequency within a frame, unidirectional GRU across time so causality holds), then a mirrored decoder with skip connections.

Published performance, for calibration: on VCTK-DEMAND it reaches PESQ 2.87, STOI 0.940, SI-SNR 18.83 dB with 0.02 M parameters. On the DNS3 blind set it scores DNSMOS BAK 3.90 but SIG 3.00 — against a noisy input SIG of 3.20. It reduces speech quality while improving background suppression, which is exactly the over-suppression failure mode this project exists to make visible.

Two findings from H-GTCRN worth applying directly: apply the mask to the original noisy input, not to Aux-IVA's cleaned output, because separation adds its own distortion and masking the raw signal avoids compounding it; and feed the network both the separated-speech and the separated-noise streams — the largest single gain in their ablation, though the paper notes it costs a slight SIG degradation.

08

Deep filtering

below ~5 kHz only · order 8 complex filter

A coarse per-band mask smears fine detail. Voiced speech has harmonic structure — regular energy peaks at multiples of the pitch — and one gain per band flattens it, which is what makes over-processed speech sound robotic.

Deep filtering fixes this. Instead of one gain per time-frequency point, it predicts a small complex filter combining several neighbouring points, which rebuilds the harmonic structure a plain mask destroys. DeepFilterNet2 applies it below 5 kHz; IS³ uses order 8 up to about 6 kHz, applying only real-valued filtering above that. Restricting the band is what keeps the cost down.

Residual stage — optional

~20 lines · must earn its latency

A final classical LMS adaptive filter to catch what the learned path leaves. Cheap, textbook, about twenty lines of code, and it handles slow drift a trained model cannot anticipate — a loosening earcup, a changing seal, the wearer moving their head.

It is genuinely optional. Whether it earns its latency is a measurement, not an opinion.

Wiring correction — audit F2

The current architecture diagram drives this block from the Mic 3 in-ear error signal and then sends the result to the radio. That cannot work: Mic 3 measures what the wearer hears, and the transmitted voice never passes through the wearer's ear. Mic 3 and its error loop belong to Lane A. If a residual stage stays in Lane B it must be driven by Mic 2, the noise reference — which is Widrow's original two-input canceller, and is correct.

11

ISTFT — back to sound

FFT again

The inverse Short-Time Fourier Transform. Turns the processed picture back into a waveform using overlap-add: each processed slice is windowed and summed into the output with the same overlap used on the way in, so with the right window and hop the overlaps sum to exactly one and the joins are inaudible. Because the complex form was kept throughout, phase is intact and the output does not sound smeared.

Perfect reconstruction through STFT → ISTFT with no processing in between is the third thing to build in the C++ engine, before any DSP. If that round-trip is not numerically exact, nothing built on top of it can be trusted.

And then the part that is not ours

The enhanced waveform does not reach a human directly. A tactical radio transmits MELPe — the NATO STANAG 4591 vocoder — at 2400, 1200 or 600 bits per second. That coder does not transmit sound; it transmits a parametric description of speech (pitch, voicing, spectral envelope) and re-synthesises it at the far end.

This matters three ways, and none of them is currently in the design:

  • The metrics are measured in the wrong place. PESQ and STOI score the denoiser's output; the listener hears the vocoder's output. Artefacts PESQ barely notices — musical noise, a smeared harmonic structure, a mis-tracked pitch across an interpolated gap — can corrupt exactly the parameters MELPe transmits.
  • MELPe already has its own noise pre-processor. Two suppressors in series can fight each other. That is a real engineering question with a measurable answer, and nobody at SIH will have asked it.
  • It is narrowband, which reopens the question of why the engine runs at 48 kHz and how much of block 8's harmonic reconstruction survives to the far end.
The cheapest original experiment available

Put a vocoder stage into the evaluation chain — an open MELPe or Codec2 implementation is enough — and report every metric twice: before the vocoder and after it. If the ranking of two methods flips across the vocoder, that single table is a more original contribution than anything else in the plan, and it is exactly the question a DRDO communications evaluator asks first.

The staggered-gain array

Set the gain for the blast and the mechanical detail disappears into the noise floor; set it for the mechanical detail and the blast hits the rails. So two channels are recorded 18–24 dB apart in sensitivity, sample-aligned:

  • ch1 HOT — captures the reverberation tail and the quiet detail. Expected to clip on the blast.
  • ch2 COLD — its only job is to catch the peak unclipped.

validate.py --array understands this: clipping on the hot channel is not a failure as long as the cold channel holds the peak. Without that flag every take would fail and the operator would learn to ignore warnings — which is the real failure mode that flag exists to prevent.

The signal chain, and the one constraint software cannot fix

mic --XLR--> [pad] --> preamp / USB interface --> laptop

                      +48 V phantom: a condenser needs it, a dynamic does not

Microphone maximum SPL is the binding constraint. A 3.5 mm electret tops out around 100–120 dB SPL; a blank pistol at 1 m is 140–155 dB. Once the capsule or its internal FET saturates, no pad and no gain change downstream recovers the waveform. Only distance helps. Check the microphone's published "Max SPL" against the source before trusting any recording of it.

Setting gain without live fire

Gain cannot be adjusted mid-string, and an averaging meter under-reads a gunshot peak by 30 dB or more. The procedure:

  1. Gain near minimum, pad ON.
  2. Fire a proxy — balloon pop or firecracker — at the real distance and angle.
  3. Read the true sample peak with monitor.py or record.py --meter, which shows peak with a hold rather than an average.
  4. Target −18 to −12 dBFS on the proxy.
  5. Back off further, because the real source is louder than the proxy.

Too little gain costs noise floor. Too much destroys data permanently. At 24-bit, a −20 dBFS peak is perfectly fine.

The second validation loop — genuinely publishable-shaped

Sound pressures add linearly in air, so at Mic 1: Type B (voice only) + Type C (gun only) ≈ Type A (both). All three sides are real recordings. Run analyze.py on B + C and on A, compare the 16 metrics, and you test three things at once:

1. Is additive mixing valid here? Everyone in speech enhancement assumes it; almost nobody checks it with real paired recordings. 2. Does the clipping model work? The mic saturates on the gunshot, so B+C will not equal A wherever clipping occurred — and the size of that difference is a measurement of the saturation nobody models. 3. Is the SNR bookkeeping right? The levels of B and C are known independently.

It costs nothing extra — just the discipline of recording all three types at the same geometry, without moving anything between blocks 5 and 6. Do it at a minimum of two distances so the result is not a single point.

What must be verified before range day

Six checks, all of which can silently ruin the trip if skipped. Do them the day before, not at the range.

  1. Are the two channels actually different? When the wireless kit was tested earlier with a single transmitter, the two channels came back bit-identical — one mono source duplicated. With two transmitters they should differ, but that must be proved. Speak into one transmitter only and check the channels diverge. If they do not, the receiver is summing to mono and the entire two-mic plan collapses to one microphone with extra steps.
  2. Are the channels sample-aligned? Put both transmitters in the same place, clap, and measure the sample offset. It should be near zero. Whatever it is, that is the fixed radio offset to subtract from every later measurement. Repeat at the end of the session to check for drift.
  3. Gain must be set per channel. Mic 2 sits near a 140–155 dB source, Mic 1 near a 70–85 dB voice. That is a 60–80 dB difference; they cannot share a setting. Log both and never touch either once shooting starts — a gain change voids the calibration for that channel.
  4. Calibration is per channel. Two channels, two gains, two microphones: two calibration tones, two scale factors.
  5. Wireless range and interference. Check the link holds at the full distance before committing to that geometry. Any dropout on the reference channel destroys the alignment for that take.
  6. Run the self-test on the actual range laptop. selftest.py must print ALL CHECKS PASSED. On Windows this currently fails for an encoding reason unrelated to the audio — see audit F11 — so verify it on the machine that is actually going.

The claim being made later is "synthetic matches real". That needs a tolerance, and the tolerance is the real thing's own natural variation — its standard deviation. But the standard deviation is itself an estimate, and it is poor when computed from few samples. Verified by Monte Carlo:

At three shots the tolerance is itself ±46 % uncertain, which makes both "it matches" and "it does not match" unfalsifiable. Never fewer than 10; 15 for the core condition. Two guns at two distances beats four guns at one distance. Propagation physics is well understood, so a generator that gets 5 m and 10 m right from one model has captured physics rather than memorised a waveform. A third and fourth source add breadth by stealing repeats from the conditions that carry the argument.

An arithmetic collision — audit F12

plan.py reserves 30 % of each condition as holdout, and the generator is validated against the holdout only, correctly, to avoid circularity. But 15 core shots minus 30 % leaves four or five holdout shots — squarely in the ±34 to ±46 % band the table above calls unfalsifiable. To get ten or more in the holdout you need roughly 33 shots per core condition. Pick one and write it down: shoot more, or state the resulting tolerance honestly as wide. A stated wide tolerance is defensible; a tolerance nobody computed is not.

The algorithms

Event detection — dsp.find_events

Locating each shot in a continuous take:

  1. High-pass at 200 Hz. Without it a wind gust reads as an onset.
  2. Moving-RMS envelope, 5 ms window with a 1 ms hop, computed by cumulative sum rather than a framed gather — O(n) with one temporary array. On a 60 s file at 96 kHz that is about 46 MB instead of 230 MB, and it is why validate.py stays inside its ten-second budget on a laptop running on battery.
  3. Adaptive threshold: max(floor + 12 dB, peak − 40 dB), where the floor is the 20th percentile — robust to the events themselves.
  4. Spectral-flux confirmation in a ±25 ms window around each candidate. A real impulse produces a broadband spectral jump; a gust or a gain change does not. Candidates below a normalised flux of 0.25 are dropped.
  5. Walk back from the peak to the threshold crossing — that is the true onset, and where the cut goes.

Minimum separation is 0.30 s by default. Full-auto bursts need a smaller value and manual review.

Impulse response — the Farina sweep

An exponential sine sweep from 20 Hz to 20 kHz over 10 seconds:

x(t) = sin( ω₁·L · (e^(t/L) − 1) ),   L = T / ln(ω₂/ω₁)

The matched inverse filter is the time-reversed sweep with a −6 dB/octave amplitude envelope e^(−t/L), normalised so that conv(sweep, inverse) peaks at exactly 1.0. gen_signals.py verifies this at generation time and reports the peak-to-sidelobe ratio — above 40 dB is required, and it typically comes out around 54 dB. Deconvolution is a linear zero-padded FFT multiply, then the impulse response is trimmed to 5 ms before the direct sound. A balloon pop with direct windowing is the fallback for when wind or traffic ruins the sweep.

RT60 — Schroeder backward integration

EDC(t) = 10·log₁₀ ( ∫ₜ^∞ p²(τ)dτ  /  ∫₀^∞ p²(τ)dτ )

A straight line is fitted between −5 and −25 dB (T20) and between −5 and −35 dB (T30), then extrapolated to a full 60 dB of decay. The r² of the fit is reported: below about 0.98 it is not trustworthy. Computed per octave band from 62.5 Hz to 8 kHz.

Calibration — turning numbers into Pascals

p_rms = 20 µPa × 10^(SPL/20)
scale = p_rms / rms_fullscale        [Pascals per full scale]
pressure = sample × scale            [any later file]

Sanity-checked: a full-scale SPL outside 100–180 dB is flagged as implausible. Changing the recorder gain voids the calibration — record a fresh tone at the new setting.

Without this step you can say "this is 6 dB louder than that". You can never say "this is 152 dB SPL", and you cannot compare with anyone else's measurement or with a physics-based model, because they have absolute units and you do not.

Why wind is measured only between impulses

Gunshots have huge genuine low-frequency content. Measuring sub-50 Hz energy across the whole file reports the shot, not the weather. The check therefore looks only at the gaps.

Why handling noise is separated by spectral shape, not loudness

Two traps here, both found by trying the obvious thing first:

  • Thresholding on a low percentile does not work. Low-passing to 100 Hz leaves a narrowband signal whose Rayleigh envelope naturally swings over 15 dB, so a relative-prominence test fires constantly on quiet ambience. On real ambience the envelope crest is about 12 dB while a knock is 60 dB or more — so the test has to be crest above the median, not above the floor.
  • Excluding candidates that coincide with a detected event does not work either, because a loud enough thump is detected as an event and therefore excludes itself.

The honest discriminator is spectral shape. Measured on this data, a gunshot sits at −2 to −16 dB LF/HF ratio and a stand knock at +33 dB, so a +10 dB threshold splits them with roughly 20 dB of margin either side.

Why bandwidth needs two signatures, not one

Found on real hardware: a 44.1 kHz microphone accepted a 96 kHz request and the operating system silently upsampled. The header was correct, every other check passed, and everything above about 21 kHz was digital zero. The file was worthless and looked perfect.

Detecting this by "where does content end" produces false positives, because a genuinely dull source also ends early. What a resample leaves is two signatures together:

  • Numerical emptiness — interpolation puts exactly zero energy above the original Nyquist, while any real recording has microphone and converter noise filling the band all the way up.
  • A cliff — the drop is near-vertical, where a dull source rolls off gently.

Measured here: a real upsample gave −135 dB with a 62 dB cliff; a merely low-passed but genuine file gave −81 dB and 19 dB. An earlier single-signature version failed the low-passed file — precisely the false positive that teaches an operator to ignore warnings.

Why dropouts are detected as exact zeros

A real microphone never outputs an exact zero; there is always self-noise and dither. A run of exact zeros means a noise gate or dropped buffers. Found on a real take: a Bluetooth headset gated 8.3 % of a 33-second recording into silence, including a single 580 ms hole, and the file passed every other check while sounding audibly chopped.

The offline pipeline

What plays the role of a backend. There is no server and no API — there is a workstation pipeline that produces one file, and that file is the only thing that ships.

"Where is your backend?" is a question that will be asked, and the answer is worth having ready: the backend is the offline pipeline, and its output is model.onnx. Everything from data generation to quantisation lives here. None of it runs on the device.

Stage 1 — Real data acquisition

Covered in full in section 06. One trip, 96 kHz / 24-bit, calibrated to absolute Pascals, with the range impulse response captured before anything else that can be shortened.

Stage 2 — The synthetic generator

The real recordings are the reference; the generator produces the training material. The chain, in order — and most naïve implementations stop at step 2, which is why they sound synthetic:

  1. Sample source parameters — peak overpressure Ps, positive-phase duration T₀, decay coefficient b — from a distribution fitted to the measured fit set.
  2. Generate a Friedlander waveform: p(t) = Ps·(1 − t/T₀)·e^(−b·t/T₀) — instantaneous rise, exponential decay, then a negative underpressure phase.
  3. Apply directivity by azimuth. A blast is not a point source; it is much louder in front of the weapon than behind it.
  4. Apply spherical spreading over distance — 6 dB per doubling.
  5. Apply atmospheric absorption, which depends strongly on temperature and humidity, and which is why those fields are logged.
  6. Add the ground reflection — delay from the logged geometry, coefficient from the ground surface. This is what produces the comb filtering visible in the spectrum.
  7. Convolve with the measured range impulse response, or an outdoor propagation model.
  8. Layer the mechanical action sounds recorded separately at close range. Almost nobody records this layer, and it is what makes a synthetic shot sound like a weapon rather than a bare pressure pulse.
  9. Pass through the device chain — clipping, AGC, converter quantisation, and the vocoder.

Published anchors worth checking your output against: a .22 pistol is roughly 146 dB at 1 m with a peak near 750 Hz; a .357 Magnum around 155 dB with a peak near 400 Hz. Peak frequency shifts down as calibre grows.

The device chain, modelled explicitly

Firearms exceed 154 dB at 3 m; consumer microphones saturate around 120–130 dB. The field microphone will clip. So model it rather than pretending otherwise:

  • Hard and soft clipping at configurable thresholds, calibrated against the deliberately-clipped range recordings.
  • AGC with attack and release, so the pumping behaviour is represented.
  • Microphone self-noise floor and converter quantisation.
  • A low-bitrate vocoder (MELPe or AMBE class) simulating the radio link downstream of the denoiser.

Calibrating the clipping model against real clipped recordings is what makes it defensible rather than guessed — and it is the direct answer to the gap where every published paper models loudspeaker saturation on the output side and nobody models microphone saturation on the input side.

Validating the generator

Measure the synthetic output with the same engine that measured the real audio, and compare against the holdout split only. session.py verify confirms the engine has not changed since the reference was frozen.

The comparison happens on features.json, not on audio. Audio is the source; two JSON files are what get compared.

Everything generated on the fly at train time from a seeded config — no pre-rendered mixture files. That keeps the repository small, makes every experiment reproducible from a config hash, and allows SNR and room response to be resampled every epoch.

The largest dataset gap in the project — audit F8

Under 80+ dB of noise, people involuntarily change how they speak: higher pitch, more level, shifted spectral tilt. That is the Lombard effect, and systems trained on neutral read speech lose roughly 5 dB when tested on Lombard speech — a gap that persists even after level normalisation.

The entire use case here is a person shouting under fire. Aim for at least 20 % Lombard content in the training mix. Lombard GRID recorded each of its 54 speakers in both conditions, with the Lombard condition induced by 80 dB SPL speech-shaped noise over headphones.

Also worth doing: boom-mic emulation. Clean corpora are recorded at about 30 cm; a headset boom microphone sits at 2–5 cm. Apply a low-shelf boost for the proximity effect, light saturation, and occasional plosive bursts before mixing, or the model learns the wrong voice.

The augmentation — where the novelty is

  1. α-stable noise instead of Gaussian, α ≈ 1. The α parameter controls tail heaviness: α = 2 is Gaussian, α = 1 is Cauchy, smaller means more violent spikes. Justified by the Generalized Central Limit Theorem. Using several separate α values beats blending them into one mixture.
  2. Below the α = 0.5 floor, and let it clip. The α-stable authors excluded α < 0.5 because roughly a third of their image pixel values ended up clipped, which they judged to compromise reliability. For us clipping is the physics — a microphone has a maximum SPL and a gunshot exceeds it every single time. We use the regime they excluded, for the reason they excluded it. They also note that truncated α-stable noise still beat truncated Gaussian, so the effect survives clipping.
  3. Input-side saturation, mirroring Deep ANC's scaled-error-function loudspeaker model at the opposite end of the chain.
  4. Structured bursts, not i.i.d. spikes. Their noise is independent sample by sample; a real gunshot has structure over tens of milliseconds — sharp attack, specific spectral shape, exponential decay, reflection tail. Generate heavy-tailed arrival and amplitude but with a measured envelope.
  5. γ derived from target SNR rather than swept blindly. Choosing the dispersion parameter is an open problem in images because there is no natural scale; in audio there is one, and it is SNR.
  6. A wide spread of room responses, near-anechoic through hard-walled, reported separately rather than averaged.
  7. HRTF-based head shadow instead of a flat scalar. The closest prior work scales the secondary channel by a random −10 to 0 dB; real head shadow is strongly frequency-dependent, and their own figure shows it varying across frequency.

Stage 5 — Compression and export

  • Structured pruning. Remove whole groups of weights — a full convolution kernel, a whole column of a recurrent matrix — rather than scattered individual ones, because hardware can actually skip a group while scattered zeros still get multiplied. Use sparse group lasso with per-tensor sensitivity analysis, then prune and fine-tune iteratively. Reference result: 290.44 K → 103.07 K parameters over six iterations, with STOI down 0.72 %, PESQ down 0.05 and SNR down 0.53 dB at 0 dB input.
  • Quantisation. The study the reference work never did. INT8, both post-training and quantisation-aware. Report the interaction explicitly: prune-then-quantise is not the same as quantise-then-prune, and speech enhancement is sensitive to reduced precision because it manipulates fine spectral detail.
  • The bonus experiment. α-stable training produces measurably sparser models — 6.16 % and 7.13 % versus 3.02 % on MNIST-C, 59.49 % versus 51.62 % on CIFAR10-C — and nobody has pruned one. Train two identical models, one Gaussian-augmented and one α-stable, prune both with the same scheme, and plot quality against parameter count. One experiment, two deliverables: better impulsive robustness and better edge compression from a single training change.
  • Export. PyTorch → torch.onnx.export with a pinned opset → ONNX Runtime on ARM, or TensorRT if the board turns out to be a Jetson, or TFLite Micro / CMSIS-NN for the microcontroller argument.

The silent failure that costs a week

The C++ implementation produces slightly different numbers from the Python one it was trained as — a different FFT scaling, a different window, a mismatched ERB band edge, a normalisation constant off by one. Nothing crashes. The model just quietly performs worse than it did in training, and you spend a week blaming the model.

The defence is a numerical parity test: fix a small set of input WAVs, run the full Python pipeline dumping every intermediate — after normalisation, after STFT, after the model, after ISTFT — run the C++ pipeline on the same inputs dumping the same points, and assert agreement to a stated tolerance at every stage. Stage-by-stage matters: if only the final output is compared, a normalisation error and an inverse-STFT error can cancel out on the test file and diverge everywhere else. Build it before the C++ path is finished, not after.

The single most important calibration in the project — audit F1

The design advocates working at −12.5 to −2.5 dB SNR, "the regime gunfire actually produces". That reasoning is sound. But at those SNRs, H-GTCRN — the paper this architecture is built on — reports PESQ 1.39 / 1.58 / 1.71 and STOI 0.72 / 0.79 / 0.82. The targets are 2.5 and 0.85. GTCRN on VCTK-DEMAND, at much friendlier SNRs, reaches PESQ 2.87 and STOI 0.940. The closest dual-mic system reaches STOI 0.876, PESQ 2.56 and SNR 8.61 dB on babble.

Nothing in the sixteen papers reaches SNR > 15 dB, and nothing reaches PESQ 2.5 below 0 dB input SNR. This is not a reason to abandon the low-SNR regime — it is a reason to stop treating the three numbers as a single pass/fail gate. Report them as curves against input SNR from −15 to +20 dB, state where you cross each target, and say plainly that below that point no published lightweight system meets them either. Then settle whether "SNR > 15 dB" means output SNR or improvement, state your reading in one line, and report both — improvement from −10 dB is a completely different claim from an absolute 15 dB output.

Why SI-SDR and not SDR — the citation the argument was missing

Le Roux et al. showed that as a growing proportion of frequency bins are deleted from a signal, SDR stays between 10 and 15 dB and actually increases at 40 % masking, while SI-SDR decreases monotonically. Deleting frequency bins is exactly what a mask-based denoiser does, and what BMRI's interpolation does in the time domain. That paper is sitting unused in docs/research/, and it gives three things the evaluation currently lacks: a published reason that a single SNR number hides over-suppression, a reason to report SI-SDR rather than SDR, and a principled way to resolve the PS's ambiguous "SNR > 15 dB".

Why DNSMOS earns its place — and why its targets need moving

DNSMOS P.835 splits SIG (speech quality alone) from BAK (background quality alone) on a 1–5 scale, without needing a clean reference. That split is what makes over-suppression visible: if the filter kills the gunshot (high BAK) but drops SIG, the metric flags that the device is degrading command clarity. A single SNR number hides that completely, and over-suppression is the exact failure the PS's intelligibility requirement exists to prevent.

The narrative — "we showed the standard metrics under-report impulsive damage, so we propose two that don't" — is a genuine measurement contribution of exactly the kind DRDO respects, and it needs no hardware, no range data and no trained model.

The gaps we build on

Each one is a place a published method stops short of what the PS demands. Every claim is checkable against a specific section or table — verify before it goes on a slide.

11.1 — There is no impulsive noise anywhere in Deep ANC

Its test noises are engine, factory, babble and speech-shaped noise from NOISEX-92, in every result table. All four are stationary or close to it. Engine noise hums, factory noise grinds, babble is a steady wash, and SSN is literally noise shaped like the average speech spectrum. Not one gunshot, not one door slam, nothing with a sharp attack. Training used a sound-effects library that probably contained transients, but nothing was ever tested on them.

The foundational deep-ANC paper does not address the noise class our sponsor names first.

What we do: run the test the authors never ran, and report the collapse honestly.

11.2 — Delay compensation quietly assumes the noise is predictable

Deep ANC buys back latency by predicting the anti-noise M frames ahead. With 20 ms frames and a 10 ms hop, each frame of prediction buys 10 ms. Its cost on engine noise: −11.07 dB with no prediction, −9.60 dB at one frame, −7.93 dB at two. Roughly 1.5–1.7 dB lost per 10 ms bought back — on periodic noise.

Prediction only works if the future resembles the past. A gunshot is by definition unpredictable. So the mechanism that makes deep ANC real-time-viable should degrade far worse on impulsive noise than 1.5 dB per frame — possibly to nothing. Nobody has measured this.

What we do: replicate that table with impulsive noise substituted in, and plot both curves on the same axes. And it dictates the architecture: if you cannot predict a gunshot, you must detect and react instead.

11.3 — RMS normalisation is formally invalid for impulsive noise

The dual-mic paper rescales every mixture so the waveform RMS equals 1. Its authors are honest that this is not causal and that deployment would need causal AGC — but RMS is a second-moment statistic, and for α-stable noise with α < 2 the variance is infinite. You cannot normalise heavy-tailed audio by RMS in any principled way. The preprocessing breaks before the model runs.

What we do: percentile scaling first, then FLOM, then causal AGC. Half a day of work; a real defect in the closest prior system; explainable in thirty seconds.

11.4 — Microphone-side clipping is modelled by nobody

Deep ANC carefully models loudspeaker nonlinearity with a scaled error function at four levels, trained and tested. Good work, all of it on the output side. Nothing models what happens when the input overloads — even though a 140 dB blast arrives at the converter already flat-topped.

What we do: extend their nonlinearity framework to the input side, same spirit and opposite end of the chain, and demonstrate with deliberately clipped recordings we made ourselves. validate.py records precisely which events clipped and by how much.

11.5 — The α-stable clipping constraint, inverted

At α below 0.5, roughly a third of the α-stable paper's image pixel values ended up clipped, which they judged to compromise reliability — so they set 0.5 as a lower bound. For them clipping was an artifact of squeezing unbounded noise into a bounded pixel range: a nuisance to design around. For us clipping is the physics. They also note that truncated α-stable noise still beat truncated Gaussian, so the effect survives clipping; it only complicated their analysis.

What we do: generate below their floor deliberately, let it clip, and train the model to reconstruct through it. We use the regime they excluded, for the reason they excluded it — and can point at their sentence to explain why the constraint does not apply to us.

11.6 — α-stable augmentation has never touched audio

The authors say so in their conclusion: noise substantially affects audio, video and text, and extension is future work. Their four datasets are two image sets and two time-series sets. Not one audio experiment. And everything is classification; enhancement is regression, which they also list as unexplored.

What we do: be first — but do not overclaim. Frame it as a hypothesis with a Gaussian-augmented baseline alongside. If it helps, we extended a published method to a new task class. If it does not, we have an honest negative result with an explanation. Assuming it works and quietly not checking is the failure mode.

11.7 — α-stable training produces sparser models, and nobody pruned them

Reported as a side effect: 6.16 % and 7.13 % sparsity versus 3.02 % for Gaussian on MNIST-C; 59.49 % versus 51.62 % on CIFAR10-C. 11.9 — The three cross-paper findings

Visible only when all the papers are read together. None of them cites the others in the relevant way.

A. Deep ANC's latency trick and the α-stable noise model are in direct conflict. Deep ANC buys latency back by prediction; prediction needs predictability. The α-stable premise is that impulsive noise is heavy-tailed — dominated by rare, extreme, isolated events, which are by construction the least predictable thing in a signal. The mechanism that makes deep ANC real-time-viable should fail exactly on the noise class the PS is about. Measured, this is the intellectual spine of the project.

B. The dual-mic preprocessing is mathematically undefined under the α-stable noise model. RMS is a second moment; for α < 2 the variance is infinite. The closest published dual-mic system has a preprocessing step that is not merely fragile but formally invalid for our noise class — and the fix is cited inside the other paper.

C. Both enhancement papers use squared-error losses, the wrong estimator here. Squared error is maximum-likelihood when the residual is Gaussian. Under heavy tails it is dominated by outliers — one impulsive sample drags the gradient more than a second of speech. L1 is better, still not matched. A loss built on fractional lower-order moments would be principled, and is the stretch goal.

Two cautions carried from the gap analysis

Verify before you cite. Sections and tables are pointed to rather than reproduced. Open each one and confirm before it goes near a slide. If a number in the notes disagrees with the paper, the paper is right.

A gap in the literature is not automatically a good project. Sometimes nobody did a thing because it does not work, or because it does not matter. Gaps 11.2, 11.3, 11.5 and 11.7 are defensible as genuinely valuable. The rest are worth checking before committing weeks.

What DRDO actually wants

A sponsor read. Partly inference from how DRDO and its funding schemes operate rather than a published SIH rubric — treat it as a lens, not a checklist.

DRDO is a government R&D organisation under the Department of Defence Production. Its institutional instinct is qualification, not demonstration. A working demo answers "does it do something". DRDO's question is "can this be specified, tested, reproduced, made in India, and fielded by someone who has never met you."

It thinks in Technology Readiness Levels

DRDO's Technology Development Fund exists specifically to raise technologies "from TRL 3 onwards" to tri-services specifications, and iDEX Prime targets TRL 4 and above with grants up to ₹10 crore. A hackathon entry is TRL 3–4.

The winning move is not to pretend you are at TRL 7 — it is to say precisely where you are and what the next two levels cost. A slide reading "we are at TRL 4; TRL 5 requires environmental qualification per JSS 55555 and a 40-hour field trial; here is the plan" reads as competence. A slide claiming a deployable soldier system reads as naïve.

It thinks in standards and limits, not benchmarks

An acoustics evaluator has MIL-STD-1474 in their head, not VCTK-DEMAND. That standard requires impulse peak levels below 140 dB at the ear, protected or unprotected — while few weapons produce below 150 dB and many exceed 180. That is the frame your project will be heard in, and your measured 140–155 dB muzzle blast figure lands inside a standard they already know.

Equally: to a defence evaluator, "speech intelligibility" has a formal meaning — ANSI/ASA S3.2, measured by Diagnostic Rhyme Test, Modified Rhyme Test, or phonetically balanced word lists, with human listeners. PESQ and STOI are proxies for that. You do not need to run a DRT, but naming it as the next rung shows you know there is a ladder.

It cares about indigenisation and IP

"Make in India" and Atmanirbhar Bharat are procurement policy here, not slogans. Permissive licences, no GPL, no cloud dependency, and a clear statement of what is yours are worth real marks. Our PFFFT-over-FFTW decision was made on exactly these grounds and should be said out loud. Conversely, note that DNSMOS is an Azure service — a foreign-cloud evaluation dependency is worth a sentence of acknowledgement and an offline fallback.

It rewards honest negative results

This is where the project is unusually well positioned. The gap analysis is exactly the register DRDO respects: naming where each published method stops, then measuring the collapse. "Deep ANC was never tested on the noise class our sponsor names first, and here is what happens when you do" is a stronger DRDO story than a polished demo.

The same goes for the provenance discipline. Absolute calibration, a holdout assigned before data exists, and a frozen reference that checksums the measurement engine itself are what qualification looks like at student scale.

It will ask what breaks in the field

Temperature, dust, vibration, fungus, EMI, power, and what happens when a component fails. JSS 55555 is the Indian joint-services environmental specification; MIL-STD-810 is the international analogue. You will not run these tests, but knowing they exist and naming which ones would apply is the difference between a student project and a proposal.

The operational hook worth stating early

Reporting from SOF Week 2026 raised questions about the lack of standardised tactical hearing protection among Indian special forces, and the Indian Army has only recently begun inducting DRDO-designed software-defined radios built by BEL. Your device sits precisely between those two facts: a headset that protects hearing and feeds an indigenous radio. One sentence, early, converts an academic project into a capability gap.

The one claim to rewrite before it reaches a slide — audit F3

What is already right — protect these from any rewrite

The robust-normalisation argument

RMS is a second moment; for α-stable noise with α < 2 the variance is infinite. A real, explainable defect in the closest prior system, with the fix cited inside another paper you already hold.

Freezing the measurement engine, not just the data

SHA-256 of analyze.py, dsp.py and wavio.py alongside every WAV. Almost nobody does this. It answers "how do we know your reference did not drift" before it is asked.

Assigning fit/holdout at planning time

Written into the metadata CSV before any data exists. Choosing it afterwards is precisely the circularity it prevents — F12 is a sizing fix, not an argument against the discipline.

The B + C ≈ A additivity check

Voice-only plus gun-only against both-at-once, all three real recordings. Tests additive mixing, the clipping model and SNR bookkeeping at once, for the cost of discipline at the range.

Refusing lossy audio — with measurements

Mid-band within 0.1 dB, but the 32 kHz band gone at −83 dB and the centroid shifted 248 Hz against a real σ of 1.43 Hz. A 173× shift. That is how to justify a format decision.

Licence discipline

PFFFT over FFTW on GPL grounds; no libsndfile so a broken native library cannot strand you at the range. Both correct, and both worth saying out loud to this sponsor.

The gap analysis itself

Naming where each published method stops, rather than summarising what it does, is what separates this from every other entry. The α-stable clipping inversion is the single best idea in the pack.

The real-time rules

No allocation, no locks, no exceptions, no logging in the audio callback — written down before a line of C++ exists. Student teams almost never do this, and the bug it prevents is nearly undebuggable.
